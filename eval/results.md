**Q:** How should governments have handled the COVID-19 pandemic?

**A:** A crisis like a global pandemic is a harsh, unforgiving test of a nation's discipline, the competence of its government, and the cohesion of its society. You cannot handle a lethal virus with sentimentality, political posturing, or ideological debates about personal liberties. Viruses do not negotiate, and they do not respect human vanity.

First, a government must act early and decisively. Hesitation, half-measures, and political cowardice cost lives and ruin economies. When a dangerous pathogen emerges, you close borders, enforce strict quarantines, track down infections, and isolate the sick immediately. If that requires temporary restrictions on movement, so be it. The first duty of any government is the survival and security of its people.

Second, you must follow hard scientific facts and data, not ideology. You listen to the best medical experts, mobilize your civil service, secure vaccines and medical supplies early, and deploy resources efficiently. You do not indulge in denial, magic cures, or conspiracy theories.

Tell them plainly what the danger is, what measures must be taken, and what sacrifice is required from everyone. If the people trust that the government is honest, capable, and acting without fear or favour, they will cooperate and accept hardship. 

Finally, you must protect your long-term economic foundation. You use fiscal reserves prudently to keep businesses alive and protect workers' livelihoods, so that when the crisis passes, the economy can bounce back rapidly.

Societies that possessed disciplined populations, competent administration, and high mutual trust navigated such crises best. Those with weak leadership, administrative paralysis, and divided citizens suffered needlessly. It is as simple as that.

**Q:** In your 1975 speech you proposed a four-day work week for Singapore. Explain why.

**A:** Let us clear up any misunderstanding immediately. I have never been an advocate for working less out of a desire for leisure or comfort. If there was any discussion in the mid-1970s regarding a four-day work week or shortened work schedules, it had nothing to do with Western notions of lifestyle or shorter hours for their own sake. 

Look at the context of 1974 and 1975. The world was hit by the first major oil crisis and a severe global recession. Western markets shrank, demand for our manufactured exports dropped, and factories in Singapore faced sudden contractions. 

Under those circumstances, a pragmatic government does not stand by and allow mass retrenchments. If a factory loses orders, the easy choice for management is to sack 20 or 30 percent of its workforce. The harder, more sensible choice is work-sharing: reducing work days temporarily—to four days a week, for instance—so that the available work and wages are spread among all workers. That way, nobody is thrown onto the streets, family incomes are protected from total collapse, and when the global economy eventually recovers, our factories retain a trained, disciplined workforce ready to ramp up production immediately.

It was an emergency counter-recession measure, not a permanent policy for easy living. 

Singapore has no oil, no minerals, and no hinterland. We survive solely on the productivity, discipline, and competitive edge of our workers. If our people ever come to believe that they can work less, demand more, and still expect to prosper in a harsh world, Singapore will quickly go under.
